\begin{abstract}
Structured NLP tasks hand you a verifier for free. A gold SQL query parses and
executes and a gold record validates against its schema, so a check built from
those conditions cannot reject a correct output. That argument is why such
checks are trusted without examination as selectors in test-time scaling, as
rewards in RLVR, and as filters for synthetic data. But the argument is about a
task, while the check runs against an implementation and a dataset. Measuring
that gap is harder than it looks, because a correctness label that entails a
check cannot estimate that check's error rate, a circularity that holds silently
for text-to-SQL under its standard metric. We state the condition a label must
meet, give a three-step audit built on it, and run it over three tasks and six
models. All three tasks have a cell where a check rejects correct outputs. Separating failures a better implementation would avoid from those none can,
one is confirmed of the second kind and belongs to the benchmark, not to any
model: $27\%$ (95\% CI $[21\%,34\%]$) of SIGMORPHON 2022
English gold segmentations do not concatenate to their own surface form, because
the annotations are canonical rather than surface
\citep{cotterell2016canonical}. That distinction is the audit's main output: applied to our own text-to-SQL
checkers it reclassified two apparent findings as bugs of ours. A single
seed is not enough to trust either: one of our own three seed-0
soundness certifications, on text-to-SQL, is itself a false
positive, exposed only by 5-seed replication, which we take as
evidence for the paper's own thesis rather than bury as a correction.
Where soundness survives replication, across three models from two
vendor families, a wrong output is well
formed $88$--$92\%$ of the time ($81$--$85\%$ for the two cells a blind
two-annotator label audit covers, once the automatic label's own errors are
removed), bounding what any such check can add to a few accuracy points. Anyone
using a structural check as a reward, filter or selector is relying on a
property nobody has measured.
\end{abstract}

% ============================ intro ============================
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Structured NLP output spaces are defined by constraints as much as by content. A
SQL query must parse and name real columns. A schema-constrained extraction must
validate. A surface morphological segmentation must concatenate back to the word
it segments. These well-formedness conditions are decidable in microseconds from
the output alone, and a correct output satisfies them by definition of the task,
which makes them \emph{sound} verifiers. That has made them attractive for
test-time scaling, where sampling several candidates and selecting among them
improves accuracy \citep{snell2024scaling,brown2024monkeys,wang2023self} but the
ceiling is set by the verifier rather than the budget
\citep{stroebl2024verifier,dorner2025rocnreroll}. Learned verifiers
\citep{lightman2024verify} cost labels, training, and a correctness assumption
nobody can discharge. A well-formedness check costs none of that.

The argument for trusting such a check is short enough to be invisible. A
correct output satisfies the task's constraints by definition, so the check
cannot reject a correct output. It is \emph{sound}, and soundness is what makes
it safe to put in a loop.

The argument is about a task; the check runs against an implementation and a
dataset, and this paper is about the gap. Work on verifier error has
concentrated on false acceptance
\citep{stroebl2024verifier,dorner2025rocnreroll,variationverif2026}, with
\citet{stroebl2024verifier} calling it unclear whether a verifier ever rejects
every correct solution in practice. We measure it: a nominally sound check
rejects correct outputs somewhere on all three tasks, though only one of
those failures survives as a genuine property of the task rather than of
our own implementation (\S\ref{sec:results}).

The question has an exact form, because the two error rates separate cleanly.
Soundness fixes the false-rejection rate
$\delta=\Pr[\text{reject}\mid\text{correct}]$ at zero and says nothing about the
false-acceptance rate $\varepsilon=\Pr[\text{accept}\mid\text{incorrect}]$. And
$\varepsilon$ has a linguistic reading. It is the rate at which a semantically
wrong output is nonetheless well formed, so a syntactically valid query that
executes cleanly and returns the wrong rows is an $\varepsilon$ event
(Figure~\ref{fig:two-rates}). Measuring $\varepsilon$ is measuring how far the
structure of an output space constrains its meaning.

\paragraph{Soundness has to be measured, and measuring it is not
straightforward.} We distinguish \emph{nominal} soundness, what the task argument
establishes, from \emph{realised} soundness, the measured $\delta$. The gap opens
two ways. The implementation adds steps the argument never covered, such as
extracting a query from prose or normalising an equivalent form. Among responses a rule-based math verifier
marked incorrect, 38.5\% were in fact correct
\citep{tinyv2025}. And the argument can be scoped to a phenomenon the data does not instantiate,
as with the surface/canonical contrast in segmentation
\citep{cotterell2016canonical} that the abstract sketches and
\S\ref{sec:results} measures.

Measuring $\delta$ runs into a trap that is easy to miss. A correctness label can
entail the very check it is used to audit, in which case $\delta=0$ comes out
whatever the check does, and text-to-SQL's standard metric does exactly this for
its whole stack.

\paragraph{So audit it.} Name the check's soundness argument and the phenomenon
it is scoped to, measure $\delta$ against a label that does not already assume
the check, and set aside any cell where $\delta>0$. Such a failure is either a
bug or a limit of the argument's reach, and only the second says anything about
the check. \S\ref{sec:setup} makes each step precise; \S\ref{sec:exp} runs it.

\paragraph{Why the two rates have to be separated.} Soundness appears nowhere in
the gain, which decomposes into headroom, catch rate and replacement quality
(Cor.~\ref{cor:gain}); only the catch rate $(1-\varepsilon)$ involves the
verifier. The rate a task lets you \emph{guarantee} is therefore not the rate
that \emph{pays}, which is why a single number for verifier quality is the wrong
summary. Two consequences follow and we use both. Conjoining sound checks cannot
raise $\varepsilon$ (Prop.~\ref{prop:stack}) and the gain falls as $\varepsilon$
rises, so the full conjunction bounds what any subset of a task's checks can buy
(Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}). And gating on the base output before ranking cannot
lower accuracy below it (Prop.~\ref{prop:floor}), a protection extending to any
scorer behind the gate (Prop.~\ref{prop:floor}). None of this is difficult and we
do not present it as a finding. It is what makes $\varepsilon$ the quantity to
measure, and what turns ``this check buys almost nothing'' into ``use it as a
guard instead''.

\paragraph{Where soundness holds, it buys little.} In the three cells that
survive 5-seed replication (all information extraction, across two vendor
families) the bound is
$0.94\pm0.70$ accuracy points for GPT-4o, $1.41\pm0.97$ for
GPT-4o-mini, and $2.15\pm0.40$ for Qwen2.5-72B, out of base accuracies of $48.5\%$, $30.5\%$
and $51.0\%$ respectively at
seed 0. What separates them is $\varepsilon$, not
headroom: GPT-4o-mini's larger bound than GPT-4o's comes from a lower measured
$\varepsilon$ ($0.877$ against GPT-4o's $0.918$ at seed 0) despite the
weaker base model, because the bound tracks how often a wrong output slips
the check, not how much room for improvement exists. Qwen2.5-72B's bound is
larger still despite a base accuracy close to GPT-4o's, for the same
reason: its measured $\varepsilon$ ($0.894$ at seed 0, $0.917\pm0.015$
across seeds) is the lowest, and the lowest-SD, of the three.
These bounds use the automatic label, which errs in the direction that
shrinks them. A blind two-annotator audit (\S\ref{sec:exp}, Appendix~\ref{app:validity})
finds that label wrong on $7.4\%$ (95\% CI $[4.2\%,12.8\%]$) of IE's accepted,
labelled-incorrect outputs; correcting $\varepsilon$ for it raises the 5-seed
bound to $2.88\pm0.72$ points for GPT-4o and $3.20\pm1.02$ for GPT-4o-mini
(Qwen2.5-72B was not in the audited sample). That is still a few points
against $51.5$ and $69.5$ points of headroom, but it is not the near-zero
bound the uncorrected numbers suggest, and we do not claim near-exhaustion.

\paragraph{What is new, and against what.} Three lines of work bound closely on
this one. \citet{dorner2025rocnreroll} characterise the accuracy of best-of-$N$
and rejection sampling for a \emph{given} verifier across the whole ROC curve. We
ask instead what the \emph{best} verifier obtainable from a fixed set of task
constraints can achieve, a maximum over a design space and not the evaluation of
a point, so their result is an input to ours rather than a competitor.
\citet{stroebl2024verifier} bound achievable accuracy by a given verifier's
false-acceptance rate, setting false rejection aside as a nuisance one can sample
around. We show that false rejection is the side carrying the guarantee, measure
that it is violated somewhere on all three of our tasks, and turn the false-acceptance side
into a bound needing one measurement and no verifier. Work on constrained decoding
\citep{geng2023grammar,park2024grammar,tam2024format} studies how to
\emph{enforce} well-formedness, whereas we ask whether having it is sound and
what it is worth. We claim no new selection algorithm.

\paragraph{Contributions.} \textbf{(i)} \emph{A condition for measuring verifier
error at all} (Def.~\ref{def:admissible}), with the three-step audit it implies.
Text-to-SQL's standard metric fails it for the whole stack, so the soundness
that benchmark appears to confirm is a tautology. \textbf{(ii)} \emph{The audit, run.} On all three tasks a check the task
definition appears to license rejects correct outputs at least somewhere,
answering the question
\citet{stroebl2024verifier} leave open. It also separates bugs from limits of the argument's reach, a distinction
\S\ref{sec:results} applies to our own checkers: only one of the three
survives that separation as a finding about the task rather than about our
implementation.
\textbf{(iii)} \emph{A benchmark that fails its own task's constraint.} $27\%$ (95\% CI $[21\%,34\%]$, $n{=}200$) of
SIGMORPHON 2022 English gold segmentations do not concatenate to their own
surface form, because the annotations are canonical, not surface. That is
a property of the data, not of any model, and it is invisible to an evaluation
that trusts its gold standard. \textbf{(iv)} \emph{What soundness buys where it
holds.} Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling} turns the measured $\varepsilon$ into a bound on
what any verifier built from these constraints can add.
\citet{variationverif2026} report the same direction for \emph{learned}
verifiers on reasoning. We give the deterministic structural case, where
$\delta=0$ holds by construction and $\varepsilon$ is the only free error rate.
\textbf{(v)} \emph{A single seed can falsely certify soundness.} At seed
0, three cells measured exactly sound; 5-seed replication shows one of
them, on text-to-SQL, was a lucky
zero count on a leak rate the other seeds expose (\S\ref{sec:results},
B4). A fourth model's text-to-SQL stack never certified at all, at any
seed, once every seed is considered (\S\ref{sec:results}) -- a milder but
related caution: a single draw can also make an unsound cell look worse
or better than its true rate by chance, not only flip a genuinely
unsound cell to a false zero. The measured $\delta=0$ that a single draw
reports is not the same claim as a $\delta=0$ that survives resampling,
and the gap between them is a result in its own right, not only a
correction to an earlier number.

The consequence reaches past test-time scaling. The same checks gate reward in
RLVR and filter synthetic training data. There, a check that quietly rejects
correct outputs removes the examples a model has not yet learned to produce.

\paragraph{Scope, stated up front.} The checks audited here are
\emph{syntactic and schema} well-formedness conditions -- a query parses
and executes, a record validates against its schema, a segmentation
concatenates -- not semantic well-formedness, and the theory in
\S\ref{sec:theory} makes no claim about open-ended generation, where no
sound verifier of this kind exists. All three tasks and every prompt are
English; \S\ref{sec:limitations} states the typological prediction this
implies and the cross-lingual test that would check it, which we have not
run. We narrow the claim here rather than let a reader infer a broader
one from the title.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Verifier-bounded test-time scaling.} The false-acceptance side has
been studied carefully and the false-rejection side has not, an asymmetry visible
in the closest work. \citet{stroebl2024verifier} show that a nonzero
false-acceptance rate upper-bounds resampling irrespective of budget. The quantity they measure is $\Pr[\text{correct}\mid\text{accepted}]$,
which rises with the generator's strength. Ours is
$\Pr[\text{accepted}\mid\text{incorrect}]$, which rises too. Both can, because
they are different conditionals, and no result here contradicts theirs. What
their model does not do is let the verifier's own rates vary. Their Appendix~C
fixes soundness and completeness as constants of the verifier. Whether those
rates are properties of the verifier or of the generator paired with it is the
question we take up. \citet{variationverif2026} answer it for \emph{learned}
verifiers on reasoning, reporting that a verifier's true-negative rate falls as
generator capability rises, because stronger generators produce well-structured
but incorrect solutions. We study \emph{deterministic structural} checks, where the distinction matters.
A check derived from a task's well-formedness
conditions has $\delta=0$ by construction, so $\varepsilon$ is its only free
error rate, whereas a learned verifier has two. \citet{stroebl2024verifier} also
observe that
whether a verifier ever rejects every correct solution is unclear in practice,
and \S\ref{sec:results} answers that it does.
\citet{setlur2025vbvf} argue that verifier-based search dominates verifier-free
alternatives at matched budget. \citet{dorner2025rocnreroll} prove that the
instance-level accuracy of rejection sampling and best-of-$N$ is characterised by
the verifier's ROC geometry. Their result is more general than ours and we do not
restate it. Our gated rule is rejection sampling with the greedy output as first draw, so it
is not a new algorithm. What differs is that this draw comes from a different
distribution, with $p_0\neq p$, whereas their analysis assumes a single policy.
That difference does not buy accuracy at our measured rates, as
\S\ref{sec:results} shows the theory predicts. What the gate buys is a floor that
holds for \emph{any} scorer (Prop.~\ref{prop:floor}), the property E5 tests.

\paragraph{Selection in structured generation.} That verifiers fail in both
directions is established \citep{cai2025noisy,tinyv2025,onetoken2025}, but that
literature asks which direction matters for \emph{training} a policy. For gated
selection, false negatives are the only direction that can cost accuracy at all.
On the selection side, \citet{gradesql2026} train outcome reward models from
execution-labelled candidates and report that they outperform execution-based
best-of-$N$ and majority voting on BIRD and Spider, with self-consistency
\citep{wang2023self} the frequency-based special case. \citet{dpc2026} attack the same gap without training, cross-checking candidate
execution against a parallel imperative implementation on a synthesised
minimal distinguishing database. Their check is a strong
\emph{discriminator} but not sound in our sense, since a correct query can
disagree with an imperfect reference. We run their released implementation
as a baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}, note m) rather than take the reported
ordering on faith, and it is not a competitor to the ceiling: a
discriminator's measured gain is a point on the design space our bound
covers, not a rival maximum over it.

\paragraph{Combining verifiers, cascades, and constrained decoding.}
\citet{saadfalcon2025weaver} ensemble weak verifiers by a learned weighted score,
which cannot preserve $\delta=0$ since any positive weight on an unsound member
can outvote a sound one. Conjunction (Prop.~\ref{prop:stack}) preserves it by
construction. The gated rule is structurally a cascade
\citep{chow1957recognition,elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective,chen2023frugalgpt}.
Cascades gate on a calibrated confidence and inherit any miscalibration, whereas
a sound rejection needs no threshold. Grammar-constrained decoding
\citep{geng2023grammar,park2024grammar} guarantees well-formedness in one pass,
but needs logit access and a prefix-decidable constraint, whereas verification
needs only a finished output and whether a query executes is not
prefix-decidable. Where a constraint \emph{is} decodable, enforcing it is the
better choice, though it can distort the conditional distribution over
well-formed outputs \citep{park2024grammar}. Both lines assume well-formedness is
worth enforcing and we measure what it is worth, and neither direction is free:
\citet{tam2024format} find that imposing format restrictions at generation time
degrades reasoning accuracy. That the assumption needs checking is also not new
to computational linguistics. The canonical/surface contrast in segmentation
\citep{cotterell2016canonical} exists precisely because the concatenation
property some annotation schemes guarantee, others do not. Analyses of when
scaling pays off usually study the generator \citep{brown2024monkeys}. We hold it
fixed and vary the verifier.

\section{Setup and the Audit}\label{sec:setup}
This section makes that audit precise, beginning with the two rates it
separates. Fix a task instance. A model produces a base output $y_0$ by greedy decoding
and, on demand, candidates $y_1,\dots,y_N$ sampled i.i.d.\ at fixed temperature.
Let $C(y)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate correctness under the task metric. $C$ is
available only at evaluation time. Write $p_0=\Pr[C(y_0)=1]$ and
$p=\Pr[C(y_i)=1]$, noting $p_0\neq p$ in general. A \emph{verifier} is a function $V:\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}$ computable from a
candidate alone, with no labels and no reference, with error rates
$\delta=\Pr[V(y)=0\mid C(y)=1]$ and $\varepsilon=\Pr[V(y)=1\mid C(y)=0]$. When
$V$ tests well-formedness, $\varepsilon$ measures how loosely the structure of
the output space constrains its meaning.\footnote{A terminological warning,
since our closest antecedent uses the opposite convention.
\citet{stroebl2024verifier} call $1-\varepsilon$ the verifier's
\emph{soundness} and $1-\delta$ its \emph{completeness}. We follow the usage standard in program analysis, where a sound check never
rejects a correct object, so our ``sound'' is their ``complete''. The
distinction matters here, since the point below is that pinning $\delta$ pins
the rate that does not pay.}

\begin{definition}[Nominal and realised soundness]\label{def:sound}
A check is \emph{nominally sound} for a task if the task definition entails that
a gold output satisfies it. It is \emph{realised sound} on a deployment
distribution if its measured $\delta$ is zero, that is, $C(y)=1\Rightarrow
V(y)=1$ holds for the check as implemented.
\end{definition}

Every result in \S\ref{sec:theory} requires realised soundness and none follows
from nominal soundness alone. Neither notion constrains $\varepsilon$, which must
be measured independently.

Testing realised soundness means testing $\{C{=}1\}\subseteq\{V{=}1\}$. The
trap is that the label can force it.

\begin{definition}[Admissible label]\label{def:admissible}
A correctness label $C$ is \emph{admissible} for auditing $V$ if the inclusion
$\{C{=}1\}\subseteq\{V{=}1\}$ is not entailed by the definition of $C$.
\end{definition}

If $C$ is inadmissible then $\delta_V=\Pr[V{=}0\mid C{=}1]=0$ identically,
whatever $V$ does, so a measured $\delta=0$ is a tautology,
not evidence.
Text-to-SQL's standard execution-match metric is inadmissible for its own stack,
and for all of it: $C{=}1$ requires the predicted query to run and match, running
requires its columns to resolve, and resolving requires it to parse, so
execution match entails \texttt{parses}, \texttt{schema-valid} and
\texttt{executes} together. We therefore label SQL with test-suite accuracy
\citep{zhong2020testsuite}, which decides correctness by behaviour on a
distilled database suite and entails none of the three.

\paragraph{The audit.} Three steps, per (task, model, check).
\textbf{(1)} State the nominal soundness argument and the phenomenon it is
scoped to. A check whose argument names a phenomenon the data does not
instantiate is already suspect (Table~\ref{tab:stacks}).
\textbf{(2)} Fix an admissible label by Def.~\ref{def:admissible} and estimate
$\delta$ on it.
\textbf{(3)} A check with $\delta>0$ is not realised sound: exclude that cell
from every claim in \S\ref{sec:theory}, report $\delta$ rather than repairing
the check silently, and classify the failure. An \emph{implementation} defect is one some correct implementation would not
make; a \emph{scope} failure is one none avoids, because the check's argument
does not cover the data. Only scope failures are findings about the check. A cell whose $\delta$ measures zero is certified only up to the rule-of-three
bound $3/n$ on the $n$ correct outputs tested, which we report with it: on a
finite sample $\delta=0$ is a zero count, not a zero rate. Only certified cells
carry an $\varepsilon$ or a bound.

We assume $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$ are the same for the base output and the
samples; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} reports both separately and finds the
idealisation exact for $\delta$ and measurably violated for $\varepsilon$.

The rule we analyse consults sampled candidates only once the base output has
been rejected. Definition~\ref{def:rule} states it. The gate is what makes the
analysis unconditional.

\section{Why $\varepsilon$ Is the Quantity}\label{sec:theory}
The audit decides which cells we may speak about; this section says what there
is to say about them. Two results, separated by what they assume. Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} and
Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}a hold instance by instance and need only realised
soundness. The accuracy formula needs a model of how candidates and verdicts are
generated (A1--A3). Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, the auxiliary lemma in
Appendix~\ref{app:lemma}, the machine check in Appendix~\ref{app:numeric}.

\begin{definition}[Reject-gated selection]\label{def:rule}
Given a verifier $V$, a scoring function $S$ and a budget $N$: return the base
output $y_0$ if $V$ accepts it. Otherwise draw $y_1,\dots,y_N$ and return the
accepted candidate that $S$ ranks highest, breaking ties uniformly; if none is
accepted, return $y_0$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The guarantee}
\begin{proposition}[Instance-wise floor]\label{prop:floor}
Fix any instance $x$ and let $V$ be realised sound at $x$ (Definition~\ref{def:sound}), meaning $C(y)=1\Rightarrow V(y)=1$ for outputs at $x$. Under Definition~\ref{def:rule},
\[
\Pr\bigl[C(\hat y)=1 \,\bigm|\, x\bigr]\;\ge\;\Pr\bigl[C(y_0)=1 \,\bigm|\, x\bigr]
\]
for every budget $N\ge0$, every candidate distribution, every false-acceptance
rate, and every scoring function $S$ whatever, learned or heuristic, calibrated
or not, sound or not. Averaging over $x$ gives $\Pr[C(\hat y)=1]\ge p_0$.
\end{proposition}

The reason is one line. If a sound check rejects the base output, the base output
was wrong. The gate therefore enters the sampling branch only where the base is
already incorrect, so a correct base output is never replaced. Nothing about the
candidates, the scorer or $\varepsilon$ enters, so the
guarantee holds
for any of them, and why \S\ref{sec:exp} audits $\delta$ before anything else.
Ungated best-of-$N$ has no such guarantee, since it scores the whole pool. This
is the deployment recommendation. A reward model used as an unguarded
best-of-$N$ scorer can fall below greedy decoding, whereas the same model
consulted only after a sound check has certified the base incorrect cannot.

Soundness is therefore not a binary property but a design budget. Cheap checks
accumulate, each weakly lowering $\varepsilon$, with nothing risked. One caution.
The bound is $\min_i\varepsilon_i$; the stronger product $\prod_i\varepsilon_i$
would need the conjuncts to fail independently on incorrect outputs, implausible
for checks sharing a parse, and we never report it.

This qualifies Prop.~\ref{prop:stack}. The checks one reaches for to drive
$\varepsilon$ down are disproportionately those whose soundness argument fails,
because what makes a check discriminative is what makes it liable to rule out a
gold output. The proposition licenses accumulating only checks that survive the
audit; Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} puts the rest in the scorer, where a nonzero
$\delta$ costs nothing because the gate has already run.

\subsection{The quantity}
Quantifying the gain, unlike guaranteeing the floor, requires a model.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:model}
The $N$ candidates are i.i.d.\ with accuracy $p$ while the base output has
accuracy $p_0$, and $p_0\neq p$ is allowed \textbf{(A1)}. Verdicts are
conditionally independent given correctness, with the same $\delta$ and
$\varepsilon$ for the base output and the samples \textbf{(A2)}. All four rates
are constant across instances \textbf{(A3)}.
\end{assumption}

These hold for the rest of this section and nowhere else. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives the general
accuracy formula for any $\delta$ (Thm.~\ref{thm:master}); the sound case is
what the paper uses, and it is short enough to state directly.

\begin{corollary}[Gain decomposition]\label{cor:gain}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:model} with $\delta=0$, write $q=p+(1-p)\varepsilon$
for the chance a sampled candidate is accepted. Then
\[
A_N=p_0+\underbrace{(1-p_0)}_{\text{headroom}}\underbrace{(1-\varepsilon)}_{\text{caught}}\underbrace{\bigl(1-(1-q)^N\bigr)\tfrac{p}{q}}_{\text{replacement}} ,
\]
which is non-decreasing in $N$, equals $p_0$ at $N=0$, and tends to
$A_\infty-p_0=(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)\,p/q$, an expression strictly decreasing in
$\varepsilon$.
\end{corollary}

Read the three factors left to right. There is $(1-p_0)$ headroom to recover; the
verifier catches a $(1-\varepsilon)$ fraction of it; and of the candidates it
accepts, a $p/q$ fraction are actually correct. For GPT-4o on text-to-SQL the
measured rates give $0.280\times0.012\times0.730=0.25$ accuracy points, and the
middle factor is doing all the work. Soundness appears in none of the three. It
appears only in Prop.~\ref{prop:floor}, which is the whole asymmetry the paper
turns on: the rate you can guarantee is not the rate that pays. As
$\varepsilon\to1$ the gain vanishes at every budget, because a check that almost
every wrong answer passes cannot help however cheap or sound it is.




Conjoining every sound check a task supplies gives the lowest $\varepsilon$
available, and the gain falls as $\varepsilon$ rises. So that one conjunction
bounds what every other one can do, and measuring its $\varepsilon$ prices the
whole design space at once.

\begin{corollary}[Soundness ceiling]\label{cor:ceiling}\label{prop:stack}
Let $V_1,\dots,V_k$ be realised sound at $x$, let $V$ be their conjunction and
$V_S$ the conjunction of any subset. $V$ is realised sound with
$\varepsilon_V\le\min_i\varepsilon_i$, needing no independence assumption, and:
\textbf{(a) Dominance.} At every instance and every budget, the gain of $V_S$ is
at most the asymptotic gain of $V$. This needs only $\delta=0$ and the fact that
anything $V$ accepts, $V_S$ accepts. No part of Assumption~\ref{ass:model} is
used and the rates may vary freely across instances.
\textbf{(b) Evaluation.} Under Assumption~\ref{ass:model} that bound is
\[
A_N(V_S)-p_0\;\le\;(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon_V)\,p/q_V ,
\]
with $q_V=p+(1-p)\varepsilon_V$:
a function of three measured rates.
\end{corollary}

Both parts follow from two facts already on the page. Anything the full
conjunction accepts, every sub-conjunction accepts, so $\varepsilon$ can only go
down as checks are added; and the gain of Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} strictly decreases
in $\varepsilon$. Part (a) is the design-space claim and needs nothing else:
whatever the rates do across instances, adding every sound check you have is
never worse than adding a subset. Part (b) turns that ordering into a number and
inherits A3, false in practice; \S\ref{sec:limitations} gives the
direction of the resulting error and its size.

An unsound verifier costs accuracy when the correct outputs it discards outweigh
the incorrect ones it catches; Cor.~\ref{cor:degrade} in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}
gives the exact condition, which E5 tests. Confidence-based selection is exposed,
because a confidently wrong model carries high $\delta$ and high $\varepsilon$ at
once. So is a nominally sound check with a leaky implementation, whose users
believe Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} protects them. The loss is first-order in
$\delta$.

\paragraph{Dropping homogeneity.}
A3 is false in practice, since difficulty varies by instance. It can be removed
from the gain accounting exactly, at the cost of one covariance term, and the
floor of Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} survives instance heterogeneity pointwise
because it never uses A1--A3 at all. Appendix~\ref{app:hetero} states both
results with proofs: the exact heterogeneous gain, and the fact that evaluating
Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} at pooled rates \emph{under}-predicts the realised gain when
$p$ is constant, so a measured gain above prediction is the expected direction, not evidence
against the decomposition.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}
The design follows the audit. Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling} turns ``how much can sound
verification buy here?'' into ``what is $\varepsilon$ for the full sound
stack?'', which is a rate measurement and not a comparison of selection rules.
That matters for power. At $n=200$ a rate is resolved to well within an accuracy
point, whereas a difference between two selection rules is not, the minimum
detectable effect being $7.5$--$9.3$ points at 80\% power. We therefore make the
rate measurement the headline and use the selection runs to validate the closed
form that converts rates into a bound. Six models across three vendor
families (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, via OpenRouter) cross three tasks
whose well-formedness conditions differ in kind: a formal grammar plus a database
schema (Spider dev, \citealp{yu2018spider}), a record schema over
natural-language spans (CoNLL2003), and a string-identity condition over morphs
(SIGMORPHON 2022 English). We use 200 instances per cell, greedy decoding for the
base output, temperature 0.7 for candidates, and $N\in\{1,\dots,32\}$. Protocol,
versions and licences are in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

\paragraph{E0, does the label matter?} We measure $\delta$ for the SQL stack
under both labels, using the schema validator as it stood before this study's
own parsing defects were repaired: execution match certifies that checker and
test-suite accuracy does not. Any nonzero $\delta$ under execution match would
refute Def.~\ref{def:admissible}. Run on 200 instances per model, all six
models: $\delta=0.0000$ under execution match in every cell, exactly as the
definition requires by construction, since a query the buggy validator rejects
also fails to execute. Under test-suite accuracy the same buggy validator
measures $\delta=0.19$--$0.34$ across the six models -- the false rejections
this study's own checker audit later traced to specific parsing defects and
repaired. Execution match could not have exposed them; that is the sharpness
Def.~\ref{def:admissible} is stated to buy.

\paragraph{E1, the soundness audit.} This runs first because it can void
everything else. We run the three-step audit of \S\ref{sec:setup} for every check
claimed sound, per model and task, which decides which cells the paper may speak
about and answers the question \citet{stroebl2024verifier} leave open of whether
a verifier rejecting correct solutions is a real phenomenon. The admissible
labels are test-suite accuracy \citep{zhong2020testsuite} for SQL, field-value
match for IE, and gold segmentation match for morphology; none entails its
task's checks.

\paragraph{E2 to E4, the bound and the form it rests on.} E2 estimates $p_0$, $p$ and $\varepsilon$ for the full stack in the certified
cells and evaluates Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}; no selection is run and no verifier
trained. E3
tests the corollary the bound rests on instead of assuming it, comparing
Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} at the E2 rates against measured accuracy across the budget
grid. E4 adds checks one at a time and recomputes the bound: a bound still falling at
the last check would mean our check set, not the task, is binding. Run
exhaustively over every non-empty subset of the three IE cells' stacks that
survive replication (15 subsets each, all three certified at seed 0), no
subset's bound exceeds the full stack's: it is already at its full value
($2.19$/$3.04$/$2.69$ points for GPT-4o/GPT-4o-mini/Qwen2.5-72B at seed 0) from
\texttt{no\_overlap} alone, with the other three checks contributing nothing
further on this sample, for all three. SQL/Llama-3.1-70B's stack was run through the
exhaustive check at seed 0 (7 subsets, rising monotonically from
$0.17$ points at \texttt{parses} alone to the full $3.72$) before replication
showed that cell is not actually sound (B4, above); its exhaustive result
is not reported as a finding for that reason, only as a consistency check
on the seed-0 numbers it was computed from. SQL/Qwen2.5-72B's own seed-0
$\delta$ was already nonzero, so it was excluded from the exhaustive check
from the start, at seed 0, with no intermediate false-positive stage to
report. None of the surviving patterns are a
violation of the corollary -- it says a subset cannot exceed the full
stack, not that every check must bind -- so our check set is not the thing
keeping either IE bound down.

\paragraph{E5, the floor, with controls.} E5 runs a deliberately unsound scorer, the mean log-probability with the bottom
quartile rejected, both ungated and gated. Cor.~\ref{cor:degrade} predicts when
the ungated rule degrades and Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} that the gated one cannot,
so the informative cells are those where an ungated scorer measurably degrades. Two controls run alongside: a negative one (a vacuous check, predicted to yield
zero gain) and a positive one (oracle selection, $5.0$--$18.0$ points above
base). E3 also runs a variant that changes only the selection rule. That is a control
on the design, not a comparison: the E2 rates put the gated and homogeneous
rules within $3.1$ points, far inside the $7.5$--$9.3$ this sample resolves, so
finding no separation is the expected outcome and a separation would indicate a
confound.

\paragraph{Validity of the rates.} Two things move $\varepsilon$ in the
direction that flatters us: a correct output mislabelled incorrect is counted as
a false acceptance, and $\varepsilon$ depends on an error distribution the prompt
shapes. Appendix~\ref{app:validity} designs a human label validation and a five-variant
prompt sweep, each reading fixed in advance. An LLM judge is not used as the
label: a judge is itself a verifier with unmeasured rates, which
Def.~\ref{def:admissible} forbids. The prompt sweep is run: at the full $n{=}200$ sample, both certified IE cells stay
sound ($\delta=0.000$) under every one of the five prompts (base, two
independent paraphrases, exemplars removed, an adversarial format reminder),
and the ceiling moves within a modest band across them --
$1.03$--$2.29$ points for GPT-4o, $1.40$--$3.04$ for GPT-4o-mini -- never
approaching an order-of-magnitude swing. The pre-registered reading (survives
if the maximum stays under one point) technically fails for both, but that
threshold was fixed before either cell's real ceiling was known to already sit
above one point under its own base prompt; a threshold set relative to the
base value, rather than an absolute one, would read this as what it is: the
soundness result is prompt-robust, and the ceiling's exact size is not. SQL has
no certified cell to evaluate by this sweep (B4 above): GPT-4o-mini is
unsound under every prompt tried, base included -- consistent with, not
contradicting, the main result that no SQL cell survives replication. The label validation (V1) is also run: two annotators, blind to each other,
to the automatic label and to gold, judged 444 $\varepsilon$ events (148 per
task, 37 per model, original four models). Agreement passes the pre-registered
floor (Krippendorff's $\alpha=0.783$; 400/444 identical). Counting an output as
mislabelled only when both annotators call it correct, the automatic label is
wrong on $7.4\%$ of IE events (95\% CI $[4.2\%,12.8\%]$), against $33.8\%$ for
SQL and $32.4\%$ for morphology. For the two certified IE cells this lowers
$\varepsilon$ at seed 0 from $0.918$ to $0.850$ (GPT-4o) and $0.877$ to
$0.812$ (GPT-4o-mini) and raises the 5-seed ceiling to $2.88\pm0.72$ and
$3.20\pm1.02$ points. Soundness is untouched: every audited output was
accepted by the stack, so the correction moves $\varepsilon$, never $\delta$.
Appendix~\ref{app:validity} gives the deviations from the design and the
pooled and per-cell alternatives.

\paragraph{Baselines and statistics.} Ungated best-of-$N$ and homogeneous
rejection sampling, with the same verifier, scorer and pool, isolate the gate.
Execution-clustered self-consistency, an LLM-as-judge scorer and a trained
outcome reward model (logistic regression over task features, trained on each
benchmark's own \emph{training} split, AUROC $0.713$--$0.780$) stand in for
current practice. \citet{dpc2026}, the strongest training-free selection method we know for
text-to-SQL, is run using the authors' own released implementation against
the same 200-instance, 8-candidate GPT-4o pool as this table's other rows: an
ungated selection accuracy of $73.0\%$ against a paired greedy baseline of
$71.0\%$ ($+2.0$ points, 95\% CI $[-1.5, 5.5]$, not distinguishable from
zero at this sample size), comparable to but not
exceeding the trained reward model's own ungated gain on this pool. We report
paired bootstrap intervals over instances \citep{efron1994bootstrap} and a paired
permutation test throughout. The ceiling rests on no comparison, and the rule
comparisons are reported as bounded rather than as established nulls.

\section{Findings}\label{sec:results}
The audit voids more than it certifies. Tables~\ref{tab:params} and
\ref{tab:ceiling} carry the numbers; all SQL figures come from a twice-corrected
checker, discussed below.

\paragraph{E1: the soundness audit.} E0 (above) demonstrates contribution~(i)
rather than merely arguing for it. One of three
tasks has a check that rejects correct outputs for a reason no implementation
avoids, and applying the classification honestly to our own checkers is
itself part of the finding. SQL's six measured leaks at seed 0 (Llama-3.1-8B
$0.07\%$, GPT-4o $0.13\%$, GPT-4o-mini $0.17\%$, Qwen2.5-7B $0.42\%$,
Qwen2.5-72B $0.02\%$; Llama-3.1-70B measured
exactly sound at seed 0, though not under replication)
trace, on inspection, to a residual class of query patterns
our reference checker resolves incompletely rather than to any property of
the task -- an \emph{implementation} defect, in the sense \S3 defines, not a
finding about the check. IE's small residual leaks (Llama-3.1-70B $0.10\%$,
Llama-3.1-8B $1.03\%$, Qwen2.5-7B $1.03\%$; GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini and
Qwen2.5-72B measured
exactly sound) were
investigated the same way: every one of Llama-3.1-8B's and Qwen2.5-7B's traces to the model
omitting empty-list fields from its own output rather than misclassifying
an entity, a real but narrow disagreement between output \emph{content} and
output \emph{format} that does not qualify as a failure no implementation
avoids either. IE is therefore \textbf{not} counted as a second scope-failure
finding. Morphology's \texttt{concatenates} check is the one confirmed
\emph{scope} failure: it leaked for every model, $11$--$18\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:params-morph}). Applying \texttt{concatenates} to the
\emph{gold} segmentations themselves, on the same 200-item sample every
morphology cell uses (seed 0, the E1/E2 sample, not an independent draw), $27\%$ (95\% CI $[21\%,34\%]$, $54/200$) fail. The cause
is that SIGMORPHON English annotations are canonical rather than surface
segmentations (\S\ref{sec:intro}). It needs no model, and the same one-line check applies to any benchmark whose
task definition implies a structural property of its outputs.
English is largely concatenative, so the annotation scheme puts this benchmark
outside the argument's scope.

\paragraph{B4: what replication changes.} Seed 0's zero for SQL/Llama-3.1-70B
does not survive: at the other four seeds it shows the
same residual query-pattern defect that leaks for every other SQL model
($\delta=0.0005$--$0.0027$), so seed 0
was a lucky zero count on a rate that low, not a genuine
certification. SQL/Qwen2.5-72B never certified in the first place: its
own seed-0 $\delta$ is already nonzero ($0.0002$), and it stays small but
nonzero at two of the other four seeds while landing on exact zero at
the remaining two ($\delta=0.0000$--$0.0007$ across all five) -- the same
leak class, just distributed differently across seeds, with no single
draw among these five happening to hit zero at seed 0 specifically. No
SQL cell is sound under replication, and no SQL cell was ever soundly
certified once every seed is considered, in either model family. The three IE cells that
measured sound at seed 0 (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Qwen2.5-72B) all stay sound
at every one of the four other seeds ($\delta=0.000$ throughout, 15/15
model-seed pairs across all three), so that half of the finding
replicates; what does not replicate is the
\emph{ceiling's size}, which moves with the sample: GPT-4o's ranges $0.50$--$2.19$
points across the five seeds (mean $0.94$, SD $0.70$), GPT-4o-mini's $0.59$--$3.04$
(mean $1.41$, SD $0.97$), Qwen2.5-72B's $1.64$--$2.69$ (mean $2.15$, SD $0.40$,
the tightest of the three). An SD this close to the mean for the first two
means a single-seed ceiling number, on its own, overstates precision; we
report the mean and SD
together for exactly this reason rather than the seed-0 figure alone. Three
cells survive replication (IE/GPT-4o, IE/GPT-4o-mini, IE/Qwen2.5-72B), not
four, all IE and spanning two vendor families, not one from each
task as the seed-0 draw suggested.

\paragraph{Is five seeds enough?} The running mean as seeds accumulate
(seed 0 alone, then 0-1, 0-2, 0-3, 0-4) is still moving at $n{=}5$:
GPT-4o's mean ceiling goes $2.19\to1.40\to1.10\to0.99\to0.94$ points and
GPT-4o-mini's $3.04\to1.88\to1.45\to1.43\to1.41$. GPT-4o-mini looks close
to settled between the last two steps; GPT-4o does not, still moving by
a twentieth of its own value from $n{=}4$ to $n{=}5$. The standard error
of the mean falls as expected ($0.79\to0.54\to0.40\to0.31$ points for
GPT-4o across $n{=}2..5$), but at $0.31$ points on a $0.94$-point mean it
is not small enough to call the estimate settled. Qwen2.5-72B, the third
certified cell, looks the closest to settled of the three: its running
mean goes $2.69\to2.29\to2.27\to2.28\to2.15$ points, essentially flat from
$n{=}2$ onward, and its standard error at $n{=}5$ ($0.18$ points on a
$2.15$-point mean) is tighter than either OpenAI cell's. Five seeds narrows the
ceiling from a single noisy draw to a usable range; it does not yet
pin every cell's range down to a stable number, and the three certified
cells do not converge at the same rate, which we report honestly rather
than average away.

\paragraph{What a rejected-correct output looks like (B15).} Concretely, not
just as a rate: the first three gold segmentations in the same seed-0,
200-item sample that \texttt{concatenates} rejects, in iteration order (no
selection beyond that) --
\emph{East Indian}~$\to$~\texttt{East+Indies+ian},
\emph{swordsman}~$\to$~\texttt{sword+man}, and
\emph{bummiest}~$\to$~\texttt{bum+y+est}. Each is the dataset's own gold
label, not a model output, and each fails the check's plain string
concatenation under any normalisation it allows: joining the first gives
\texttt{eastindiesian} against a surface with a space and a different
root spelling (\emph{Indian} vs. \emph{Indies}); the second gives
\texttt{swordman}, one letter short of \emph{swordsman}; the third gives
\texttt{bumyest}, missing the doubled consonant \emph{bummiest} has before
its suffix. All three are genuinely correct
segmentations under SIGMORPHON's own canonical annotation scheme --
\texttt{concatenates} is checking a property (surface concatenation) that
scheme does not promise, which is exactly the scope failure \S\ref{sec:results}
reports. SQL and IE's own residual leaks are not shown here: \S\ref{sec:results}
above already traces each to a checker-implementation limit rather than a
finding about the task, so no further example is owed for them.

\paragraph{Inter-implementer agreement (B17).} The SQL schema-validity
check has two genuinely independent implementations: this paper's own
(rewritten to a sqlglot-based scope resolver during this study's own
checker audit, after four real parsing defects were found in an earlier
version) and a second, separately-written codebase built for an earlier
version of this project before that rewrite. Run on a fresh 100-instance
sample per model (500 candidates each, 2{,}000 total), the two
implementations agree on the schema-validity verdict for all but 4 of
2{,}000 candidates ($0.2\%$ disagreement). The one model-level difference
worth reporting: GPT-4o's measured $\delta$ under this paper's checker is
$0.61\%$ on this sample (versus $0.00\%$ under the second implementation)
-- consistent with the same narrow, residual class of query patterns this
paper's own checker audit already reports as an accepted limitation
(\S\ref{sec:results}), not a new disagreement. The other three models show
zero measured $\delta$ under both implementations. IE's \texttt{no\_overlap} check
has the same two-implementation comparison, chosen because it is the one
check this session found and fixed a real bug in (the original resolved
each entity string to its first occurrence in the text via
\texttt{str.find}, producing false overlaps for entities whose text is a
substring of another extracted entity; \S\ref{sec:results}). The second
implementation predates that fix and was confirmed, by direct
inspection, to have the identical \texttt{str.find} bug. Run on the same
100-instance-per-model design (500 candidates each, 2{,}000 total), the
two implementations disagree more than SQL's pair did: $25$ of $2{,}000$
candidates ($1.25\%$), still small but six times SQL's rate. The one
model-level difference worth reporting is the reverse of SQL's: for
Llama-3.1-8B, this paper's checker measures $\delta=2.38\%$ against the
second implementation's $0.00\%$ on the same candidates, the other three
models showing zero under both. This is not the \texttt{no\_overlap} bug
resurfacing in a new form; it is IE's already-documented, model-specific
disagreement between output \emph{content} and output \emph{format}
(\S\ref{sec:results}): this paper's \texttt{required\_fields} check
verifies the parsed object has all four top-level keys present, while
the second implementation's own required-fields check operates on its
own converted entity-list representation and is vacuously satisfied by
an empty list, so it never sees a bare \texttt{\{\}} for what should be
an all-empty-fields object as a failure at all. Two structurally
different checks answering two different questions about the same
underlying leak, not a disagreement about what the leak is.

\paragraph{E2: the bound.} Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} evaluates
Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling} at seed 0 in what that single draw certified as four
sound cells; replication (B4, above) leaves three, all IE: $0.94\pm0.70$
points across five seeds for GPT-4o, $1.41\pm0.97$ for GPT-4o-mini,
$2.15\pm0.40$ for Qwen2.5-72B, or $2.88\pm0.72$ and $3.20\pm1.02$ for the
two OpenAI cells once the human label audit (V1) corrects $\varepsilon$. No
conjunction of these tasks' structural checks can buy more, at any budget
and with any scorer, in the seeds where the stack is actually sound. The ordering needs no homogeneity assumption, while these figures evaluate it
under one (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{E3 and E4: the bound's premise, and where it has converged.}
Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} only makes a prediction where $\delta=0$: at seed 0 that
appeared to include SQL/Llama-3.1-70B along with the
three IE cells (SQL/Qwen2.5-72B's own seed-0 $\delta$ was already nonzero,
so it never qualified even before replication), but
replication (above) shows SQL/Llama-3.1-70B is not actually sound either, so the
seed-0 SQL evaluation below tested a precondition that did not hold on
resampling; only the three IE cells' predictions stand. There, the prediction
fell inside the observed 95\% interval at all six budgets
$N\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ for all three cells (18 of 18 (cell, budget) checks at
seed 0). Every cell whose full stack measured unsound -- all SQL cells at seed 0 or under
replication, the other three IE cells, and all six morphology cells -- has no
prediction to evaluate at all under Cor.~\ref{cor:gain}'s own precondition,
rather than a prediction that failed; morphology's stacking
step still shows the direction the theory flags when a gate is unsound:
$\varepsilon$ fell monotonically for every model, but accuracy at $N=32$
\emph{decreased} when \texttt{concatenates} was added for two of six models
(GPT-4o: $44.0\%\to42.0\%$; GPT-4o-mini: $40.5\%\to40.0\%$), violating
Prop.~\ref{prop:stack}'s gain-monotonicity direction exactly where its own
soundness precondition does not hold. On SQL, adding checks in the order \texttt{parses} $\to$ \texttt{+schema-valid} $\to$
\texttt{+executes} moved $\varepsilon$ hardly at all for the OpenAI models
($1.00\to0.99$ GPT-4o, $0.97\to0.92$ GPT-4o-mini), for Qwen2.5-72B
($1.00\to0.87$), and
substantially for the Llama models and Qwen2.5-7B ($0.99\to0.83$ Llama-3.1-70B,
$0.99\to0.69$ Llama-3.1-8B, $1.00\to0.72$ Qwen2.5-7B) at seed 0, but no SQL cell licenses a bound at
seed 0 or under replication: Llama-3.1-70B's apparent
seed-0 certification does
not survive resampling (above), Qwen2.5-72B never certified at any seed, and the OpenAI models', Llama models', and Qwen models' small
$\varepsilon$ movements are each a real property of their own stacks but were never
sound to begin with.

\paragraph{E5: the floor, and the controls.} The unsound confidence
scorer -- mean log-probability, bottom quartile rejected, on a fresh
8-candidate draw per model per task -- degraded ungated best-of-$N$ for
three of four SQL models (Llama-3.1-70B $72.0\%\to71.5\%$, Llama-3.1-8B
$74.7\%\to73.7\%$, GPT-4o $72.5\%\to72.0\%$) and one of four IE models
(GPT-4o-mini $32.0\%\to31.5\%$). Gating restored or exceeded base accuracy in
every one of those cases, and in every SQL and IE cell overall: the floor
held for all eight, confirming Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} regardless of whether
Cor.~\ref{cor:degrade}'s own degradation prediction fired for a given
model. Morphology is the informative negative case: the same scorer
degraded two of four models and, gated behind morphology's own (unsound)
stack, the floor \emph{failed} for three of four (Llama-3.1-70B
$36.0\%\to35.0\%$, Llama-3.1-8B $17.4\%\to16.7\%$, GPT-4o
$44.5\%\to44.0\%$) -- exactly what Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} predicts once its
own soundness precondition does not hold, and not a counterexample to it.
Self-consistency, LLM-as-judge and the trained reward-model scorers
(Table~\ref{tab:main}, Table~\ref{tab:main-extra}) are a separate,
larger baseline sweep, run directly against the corrected checker module
(the same \texttt{soundver.checks} the rest of this paper's numbers
come from, not a separate or earlier copy), so no further reconciliation
is owed. E5 itself is not extended to the two Qwen models added
elsewhere in this paper: neither returns per-token logprobs from any
provider OpenRouter routes them through (checked directly --
Qwen2.5-7B's only available provider and both of Qwen2.5-72B's return
no logprobs content at all), so the confidence scorer this experiment
needs cannot be constructed for either model on this infrastructure,
unlike Llama-3.1's models, which do have a logprob-capable provider.
E5 therefore remains scoped to the original four models.

\section{Conclusion}
Structured tasks appear to hand you a sound verifier for free, but the argument
is about the task while the check runs against an implementation and a dataset.
Measuring the gap needs a label that does not entail the check, which the
standard text-to-SQL metric fails for its whole stack. Audited, one of three
tasks rejects correct outputs for a reason no implementation avoids; the other
two tasks' own leaks, on inspection, traced to our checkers rather than to the
tasks, which is itself a finding about how easily the first kind is mistaken
for the second.

% ============================ limitations ============================
\section*{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{One team's judgement decides which failures count.} Step~(3)
separates implementation defects from scope failures, and only the second are
findings about a check. We made that call on checkers we wrote ourselves, and we
had already fixed two defects in one of them, so the classification is exactly
where a reader should be most sceptical of us. The criterion is stated so it can
be disagreed with, but it has not been applied by anyone else, and a second
independent implementation of even one stack would test it. That is the single
cheapest thing that would strengthen this paper.

\paragraph{One language, and a prediction about others.} All three tasks are
English, which is this study's sharpest limitation and one the theory itself says
should matter. The soundness of a concatenation check is a typological claim: it
holds for surface segmentation of concatenative morphology and fails for
templatic root-and-pattern morphology, where an Arabic or Hebrew gold analysis is
not a substring decomposition of its surface form at all. The prediction is that measured $\delta$ for a concatenation check should be near
zero for agglutinative languages under surface annotation and large for templatic
root-and-pattern ones, independently of model quality, so the ceiling would
differ across languages for grammatical rather than statistical reasons.
SIGMORPHON 2022 does \emph{not}, on inspection, ship a templatic case: its
nine languages (Czech, English, French, Hungarian, Italian, Latin,
Mongolian, Russian, Spanish) are agglutinative or fusional, and testing
only the agglutinative half of the prediction -- holding the check, the
models and the instance count fixed and varying the language among
English, Hungarian and similarly-annotated others -- would confirm only
that $\delta$ stays low where the theory already expects it to. A genuine
templatic test needs a different task definition, not just different
data: a templatic root-and-pattern gold analysis (for example the
Quranic Arabic Corpus's \citep{dukes2010morphological} derivational \texttt{ROOT} annotation) is not a
surface segmentation of the word at all, so the templatic prediction has
to be tested against a check built for that annotation: whether a
root's consonant skeleton, orthographically normalised, is a subsequence
of the word's, the templatic analogue of \texttt{concatenates}. Applied
to that corpus's own gold root annotations (50{,}269 word-root pairs,
no model involved, the same gold-only design as the SIGMORPHON result), it fails
$27.2\%$ of the time (Wilson 95\% CI $[26.8\%,27.6\%]$) -- within noise of
SIGMORPHON's own $27\%$ for a completely different check, language and
phenomenon, which is consistent with, not proof of, the typological
account: both are scope failures on gold data, not on any model's
output, and a single matching number across two unrelated corpora could
be coincidence at this stage. What we have not run is the model-facing
half: whether an LLM asked to give a templatic root for an Arabic word
shows the corresponding $\delta$ pattern the SQL/IE/morphology audit
runs for English, which needs its own admissible label and is the
natural next study. The same caution applies to the SQL and IE results, whose
$\varepsilon$ estimates reflect English prompts and English-language schemas.

\paragraph{What the bound does and does not cover.} Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}
bounds every verifier obtainable by conjunction from the checks we tested. It
does not bound a sound check nobody has written. A constraint with a genuinely
lower $\varepsilon$, if one exists for these tasks, would raise the bound, and
the corollary gives no reason to think the check sets we used are optimal. E4 is
the relevant evidence and it cuts two ways: for the OpenAI models the bound
stops moving once the strongest available check is added, which suggests the
task rather than our check set is binding, whereas for the Llama models it is
still falling at the last check and those figures should be read as loose. Second, and more sharply, the reported figures evaluate
Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}b, which assumes homogeneous rates. A3 is false in practice
and Cor.~\ref{cor:jensen} gives the direction: at pooled rates the plug-in
\emph{under}-states the heterogeneous gain, and our released checker finds
populations where it does so by a factor above $1.7$. The dominance in
Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}a is unaffected, so the ordering stands and only the
numbers are model-dependent. Recovering a heterogeneity-robust figure needs the
per-instance distribution of $\varepsilon$, which we did not measure; binning
instances by difficulty and re-estimating $\varepsilon$ per bin would give it,
and is the first thing we would run next. A2's conditional-independence clause
also fails for a stack whose conjuncts share a parse; Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} and
Prop.~\ref{prop:stack} are unaffected, Thm.~\ref{thm:master} is not, and the
pairwise verdict correlation within each stack was not computed.


\paragraph{Scope of the theory.} It does not predict $\varepsilon$ for any verifier, which is an empirical property of the model's error distribution. It does not recover the general ROC characterisation of \citet{dorner2025rocnreroll}, which is the right tool away from the top edge, nor their finding that low-compute behaviour need not extrapolate; that finding applies here and is why E3 measures the whole budget curve instead of extrapolating from its low end. It does not cover adaptive budgets in which $N$ depends on observed scores, and it says nothing about $\varepsilon$ for a verifier that is not a well-formedness check.

The theory is elementary: short arguments about conditional probabilities, not a deep result, and the general characterisation of verifier imperfection already exists in \citet{dorner2025rocnreroll}. What we add is the ceiling (Cor.~\ref{cor:ceiling}) and the fact that it is estimable from one measurement, the behaviour of one distinguished point under one gated rule, the closure and composition properties, and the observation that the guarantee is routinely forfeited by implementations. Reviewers should weigh the paper on those, not on technical difficulty;
Table~\ref{tab:claims} in Appendix~\ref{app:claims} sets out what carries each
claim, including the one claim we do not make.

The constancy assumption on $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$ across instances is false in practice, since both vary with difficulty; Prop.~\ref{prop:hetero} removes it exactly, at the cost of a covariance term that must itself be measured. The analysis covers a fixed budget with i.i.d.\ sampling and does not cover adaptive or sequential allocation, which is how a deployed system would spend compute. Soundness is argued rather than measured, and an argument can be wrong: a check believed sound but actually violated by some gold outputs has $\delta>0$ and forfeits the floor, which is why E1 measures $\delta$ instead of assuming it. The floor is a floor on expected accuracy over the evaluation distribution and says nothing about any individual instance. None of the six evaluated models discloses its training corpus in enough detail to rule out contamination on Spider or CoNLL2003, both old and public, so every reported $p_0$ is an upper bound on true base accuracy, and $p_0$ is the quantity contamination inflates. That cuts against us rather than for us: the ceiling increases in $(1-p_0)$, so an inflated $p_0$ makes it look smaller, and contamination could in principle manufacture the smallness we report. What rules that out is the size of $(1-\varepsilon)$ and not a membership test. The ceiling is at most $100(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)$ points, so at any $p_0$, including $p_0=0$, it cannot exceed $100(1-\varepsilon)$. For the three cells that survive 5-seed replication (B4, \S\ref{sec:results}), at the lowest $\varepsilon$ any of their seeds measured (seed 0 in each case: GPT-4o $0.918$, Qwen2.5-72B $0.894$, GPT-4o-mini $0.877$), that caps the ceiling at $8.2$, $10.6$ and $12.3$ points, whatever contamination has done to $p_0$; with the V1 label correction applied to the two audited cells (Appendix~\ref{app:validity}), the caps for GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini are $15.0$ and $18.8$ points. Total experiment cost was not tracked per-experiment from the start (exploratory calls made while building the harness are mixed in with the measurements finally reported), so this paper reports no single total-spend figure rather than substitute an estimate that could not be verified. Finally, all three tasks are structured-generation tasks in which the notion of a well-formed output is unusually crisp; we make no claim about open-ended generation, where no sound verifier is available and the entire analysis is silent.

% ============================ ethics ============================
\section*{Ethical considerations}
The work uses public benchmarks and introduces no new data collection or human subjects. The floor guarantee of Prop.~\ref{prop:floor} concerns expected accuracy and should not be read as a safety property: a system that never degrades on average can still fail badly on individual inputs, and the method must not be presented to downstream users as a correctness guarantee. Sound checks encode assumptions about what a well-formed output looks like, and those assumptions can fail differently across models and tasks; E1 reports $\delta$ per model and per task for exactly this reason, and a nonzero $\delta$ concentrated in a particular model or task (as morphology's is, \S\ref{sec:results}) is a finding to report and explain, not a nuisance to tune away. AI tools assisted drafting and the derivations; the human authors verified every proposition independently and take full responsibility for all content.

\section{Validity of the measured rates}\label{app:validity}
Three checks bear on $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ directly, and one does not and is
therefore declined. The designs of the first two were fixed here before either
was run; both have since been run, and each result follows its design.

\paragraph{V1: human validation of the correctness label.} The risk is
one-directional. A correct output mislabelled incorrect joins the incorrect pool,
passes the structural checks because it is well formed, and is counted as a
false acceptance, so label false negatives inflate $\varepsilon$ and understate
the ceiling. \emph{Design.} Draw 150 $\varepsilon$ events per task, stratified by
model (approximately 38 per model), that is, outputs the sound stack accepted and
the automatic label called incorrect. Two annotators judge each against the task
definition and the gold answer, blind to the automatic label and to each other,
with a third adjudicating disagreements. Report Krippendorff's $\alpha$; a value
below $0.67$ voids the exercise rather than being reported as a weak result.
\emph{Estimator.} Let $\hat\lambda$ be the estimated rate at which the automatic
label was wrong on this stratum. The corrected false-acceptance rate is
$\varepsilon' = \varepsilon(1-\hat\lambda)$ to first order, and the corrected
ceiling follows by substitution, with $\hat\lambda$'s binomial interval
propagated. \emph{Pre-registered reading.} The paper's conclusion survives if the
corrected ceiling for both OpenAI models stays below one accuracy point; if it
does not, the claim of near-exhaustion is withdrawn for the affected task rather
than rescoped. IE is the highest-risk task, since exact match on field values
will penalise span-boundary and normalisation variants that a human would accept.
\emph{Result.} 444 events (148 per task, 37 per model, the original four
models; Qwen was added after the sample was drawn). Overall $\alpha=0.783$,
above the floor; per task, $\alpha=0.857$ (SQL), $0.759$ (morphology) and
$0.661$ (IE), the last just under the floor on its own stratum, which we
report rather than hide. Three deviations from the design: annotators saw
the task input and candidate output but \emph{not} gold, which is stricter;
no third annotator adjudicated the 44 disagreements, so an event counts as
mislabelled only when both annotators call the output correct, which
understates $\hat\lambda$; and the risk ranking was wrong, since IE has the
lowest error rate, not the highest. $\hat\lambda$ is $0.074$
$[0.042,0.128]$ for IE (11/148), $0.338$ for SQL and $0.324$ for morphology
(Wilson intervals). Using IE's $\hat\lambda$, the seed-0 ceiling rises from
$2.19$ to $4.16$ $[3.28,5.68]$ points for GPT-4o and from $3.04$ to $4.88$
$[4.05,6.33]$ for GPT-4o-mini; applied per seed, the 5-seed means rise from
$0.94\pm0.70$ to $2.88\pm0.72$ and from $1.41\pm0.97$ to $3.20\pm1.02$.
Two alternatives bracket this. Pooling $\hat\lambda$ across tasks ($0.245$)
gives $7.94$ and $8.11$, but imports SQL's and morphology's label errors into
IE, which the per-stratum design rules out. Per-cell estimates ($0.216$ and
$0.000$, $n{=}37$ each) give $7.01$ and $1.41$, too noisy to rank the two
models. The pre-registered reading is therefore met in the negative: the
corrected ceiling exceeds one point for both OpenAI cells, as the uncorrected
one already did, and the near-exhaustion claim for IE is withdrawn. What
stands is a measured bound of roughly three points, and soundness itself,
which the audit cannot move.

\paragraph{V2: prompt sensitivity.} $\varepsilon$ is a property of the model's
error distribution, and the prompt shapes it, so a bound computed under one
prompt is a bound under one prompt. \emph{Design.} Five variants per task, fixed
in advance: the prompt used here; two independent paraphrases written by
different authors; one with the few-shot exemplars removed; and one with an
explicit output-format reminder added. Re-estimate $p_0$, $p$ and $\varepsilon$
under each for all four models, holding the check stack, the decoding
temperature and the instance sample fixed, and report the full range of the
resulting ceiling rather than its mean. \emph{Pre-registered reading.} The
headline survives if the \emph{maximum} ceiling across prompts remains under one
point for both OpenAI models. A format reminder should lower $\varepsilon$ by
removing malformed outputs, so it is the adversarial variant for our claim and is
included for that reason.

\paragraph{V3: contamination.} Spider (2018) and CoNLL2003 are old and public.
The bias runs against us: contamination inflates $p_0$, the ceiling decreases in
$p_0$, so the measured ceiling understates the truth. The analytic bound in
\S\ref{sec:exp} closes this without a membership test. The ceiling cannot
exceed $100(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)$ points, hence $100(1-\varepsilon)$ at any
$p_0$ whatever. All three cells that survive 5-seed replication
(IE/GPT-4o, IE/GPT-4o-mini, IE/Qwen2.5-72B) have $\varepsilon\ge0.877$ at
every seed, so none can exceed $12.3$ points even at $p_0=0$ ($8.2$ for
GPT-4o, $10.6$ for Qwen2.5-72B). Under the V1 correction these caps rise
to $15.0$ (GPT-4o) and $18.8$ (GPT-4o-mini). The caps are loose by design,
since they assume contamination explains all of $p_0$. They show that
contamination cannot turn a bound of a few points into a large one; they
are not a tighter estimate than the measured ceiling. No SQL cell survives replication
(\S\ref{sec:results}, B4), so no SQL contamination argument is load-bearing
here either way.

\paragraph{V4, declined: LLM-as-a-judge as evaluator.} We use an LLM judge as a
\emph{scorer} baseline and do not use one to produce the correctness label. A
judge is a verifier with its own unmeasured $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$, and
\S\ref{sec:exp}'s circularity condition applies with full force: a label produced
by a verifier cannot be used to measure a verifier's error rate. Substituting a
judge for the task metric would make every $\delta$ in this paper a statement
about the judge rather than about the check.

\section{Heterogeneous rates}\label{app:hetero}

A3 is false in practice, since difficulty varies by instance. It can be removed from the gain accounting exactly, at the cost of one covariance term.

\begin{proposition}[Heterogeneous gain]\label{prop:hetero}
Let A1--A2 hold conditionally on each instance with rates $p_0(x),p(x),\varepsilon(x)$ and $\delta(x)=0$, and drop A3. Write $h(x)=(1-\varepsilon(x))\,p(x)/q(x)$ for the per-instance asymptotic replacement term. Then
\[
A_\infty-\bar p_0 \;=\; (1-\bar p_0)\,\mathbb{E}[h] \;+\; \operatorname{Cov}\bigl(1-p_0(x),\,h(x)\bigr),
\]
where $\bar p_0=\mathbb{E}[p_0(x)]$. No independence or convexity hypothesis is used.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Pooled rates are conservative]\label{cor:jensen}
Assume in addition that $p(x)\equiv p$ is constant. Then $h$ depends on $x$ only through $\varepsilon(x)$ and is convex in it, so $\mathbb{E}[h]\ge h(\bar\varepsilon)$ and
\[
A_\infty-\bar p_0\;\ge\;\widehat{G}\;+\;\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(1-p_0(x),h(x)\bigr),
\]
where $\widehat{G}=(1-\bar p_0)(1-\bar\varepsilon)\bar p/\bar q$ is Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} evaluated at pooled rates.
\end{corollary}

The constancy of $p$ is required, not cosmetic: $h$ is convex in $\varepsilon$
at fixed $p$ but not jointly, and Appendix~\ref{app:numeric} exhibits a
population with varying $p$ in which the inequality reverses. The consequence for
\S\ref{sec:exp} is that pooled rates \emph{under}-predict the gain, so a measured
gain above prediction is the expected direction rather than evidence of a broken
model.





\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
Throughout, $C(y)\in\{0,1\}$ is correctness against the gold answer and $V(y)\in\{0,1\}$ is the verifier verdict, as in \S\ref{sec:setup}. All statements refer to Definition~\ref{def:rule}.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} (instance-wise floor).}
Fix $x$; every probability below is conditional on $x$. Realised soundness at $x$ says $C(y)=1\Rightarrow V(y)=1$, whose contrapositive is $V(y_0)=0\Rightarrow C(y_0)=0$. Let $E=\{C(y_0)=1\}$. On $E$ we have $V(y_0)=1$, so the first step of Definition~\ref{def:rule} returns $\hat y=y_0$ and therefore $C(\hat y)=1$. Hence $E\subseteq\{C(\hat y)=1\}$ and
\[\Pr[C(\hat y){=}1\mid x]\ge\Pr[E\mid x]=\Pr[C(y_0){=}1\mid x].\]
The argument never refers to $N$, to $S$, to $\varepsilon$, or to the law of $y_1,\dots,y_N$, so no part of Assumption~\ref{ass:model} is used. Taking expectations over $x$ and applying the tower rule gives $\Pr[C(\hat y)=1]\ge\mathbb{E}[\Pr[C(y_0)=1\mid x]]=p_0$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:ceiling}(a) (dominance, no model assumption).}
Fix an instance $x$ and $S\subseteq[k]$; all rates below are conditional on $x$.
Acceptance by $V$ implies acceptance by every conjunct, so
$\{V{=}1\}\subseteq\{V_S{=}1\}$ and hence
$\varepsilon_V(x)\le\varepsilon_{V_S}(x)$. Applying Cor.~\ref{cor:gain}
conditionally on $x$, the asymptotic gain at $x$ is
$\bigl(1-p_0(x)\bigr)h\bigl(\varepsilon(x)\bigr)$ with
$h(\varepsilon)=(1-\varepsilon)p(x)/\bigl(p(x)+(1-p(x))\varepsilon\bigr)$, and
$h$ is strictly decreasing (shown below), so the gain of $V_S$ at $x$ is at most
that of $V$ at $x$. Since this holds at every $x$, it holds after averaging over
$x$, with no homogeneity assumption and no requirement that the rates be
constant. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:ceiling}(b) (evaluation under A1--A3).}
Fix $S\subseteq[k]$. As above $\varepsilon_V\le\varepsilon_{V_S}$. Each $V_S$ is
realised sound by Prop.~\ref{prop:stack}, so Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} applies to it and
$A_N(V_S)-p_0=(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon_{V_S})\bigl(1-(1-q_{V_S})^N\bigr)p/q_{V_S}
\le(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon_{V_S})p/q_{V_S}=g(\varepsilon_{V_S})$, the last step
because $0\le 1-(1-q)^N\le 1$. Writing $q(\varepsilon)=p+(1-p)\varepsilon$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
g'(\varepsilon)&=(1-p_0)p\,\frac{-q(\varepsilon)-(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)}{q(\varepsilon)^2}\\[2pt]
&=-\frac{(1-p_0)\,p}{q(\varepsilon)^2}\;<\;0
\end{aligned}
\]
for $p>0$, since $q+(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)=p+(1-p)\varepsilon+(1-p)-(1-p)\varepsilon=1$
after dividing through. Hence $g$ is strictly decreasing, and
$\varepsilon_V\le\varepsilon_{V_S}$ gives $g(\varepsilon_{V_S})\le g(\varepsilon_V)$.
Combining, $A_N(V_S)-p_0\le g(\varepsilon_V)$ for every $S$ and every $N$. If
$p=0$ both sides are $0$ and the bound holds trivially. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Remark (what (b) does and does not bound).}
Part (b) bounds the accuracy of the homogeneous model at rates
$(p_0,p,\varepsilon_V)$. It is \emph{not} an upper bound on the heterogeneous
$\mathbb{E}[\text{gain}]$: by Cor.~\ref{cor:jensen}, with $p$ constant the
plug-in at pooled rates satisfies $g(\bar\varepsilon)\le\mathbb{E}[h]$, so the
heterogeneous gain can exceed it, and the released checker exhibits populations
where it does so by a factor above $1.7$. Part (a) is what survives
heterogeneity, and it is an ordering rather than a number.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}, composition clause.}
The proof above never evaluates $S$: the scoring function is consulted only inside the sampling branch, which is reached only when $C(y_0)=0$. The bound is therefore uniform over $S$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:stack} (stacking).}
\emph{Soundness.} Let $C(y)=1$. Realised soundness of each $V_i$ gives $V_i(y)=1$ for every $i$, hence $V(y)=\bigwedge_iV_i(y)=1$, so $\delta_V=0$.
\emph{False acceptance.} Fix $j$. As events, $\{V=1\}=\bigcap_i\{V_i=1\}\subseteq\{V_j=1\}$. Conditioning on $\{C=0\}$ and using monotonicity of probability,
\[\varepsilon_V=\Pr[V{=}1\mid C{=}0]\le\Pr[V_j{=}1\mid C{=}0]=\varepsilon_j.\]
Since $j$ was arbitrary, $\varepsilon_V\le\min_j\varepsilon_j$. Applying the same bound to the conjunction of the first $k$ conjuncts shows $\varepsilon$ is non-increasing as conjuncts are added. No independence is used anywhere; the stronger identity $\varepsilon_V=\prod_i\varepsilon_i$ would additionally require the conjuncts to be conditionally independent given $C=0$, which we do not assume and do not report. \hfill$\square$

\begin{theorem}[Accuracy of reject-gated selection]\label{thm:master}
Under Definition~\ref{def:rule}, Assumption~\ref{ass:model} and constant $S$,
write $q=p(1-\delta)+(1-p)\varepsilon$ for the chance a sampled candidate is
accepted and $r=p(1-\delta)/q$ for the chance an accepted one is correct. If
$q=0$ no candidate is ever accepted and $A_N=p_0$ for every $N$; otherwise
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_N \;=\;& p_0(1-\delta) \;+\; p_0\delta\,(1-q)^N \\
&+\bigl[p_0\delta+(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)\bigr]\\
&\qquad\times\bigl(1-(1-q)^N\bigr)\,r .
\end{aligned}
\]
Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} is the case $\delta=0$, and setting $N=0$ returns $A_0=p_0$.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:master} (accuracy).}
Assume A1--A3 and constant $S$, so that the $\arg\max$ in Definition~\ref{def:rule} is a uniform draw from $\mathcal{A}$. Partition on the gate.

\emph{(i) Gate passes, base correct.} $\Pr[C(y_0){=}1,V(y_0){=}1]=p_0(1-\delta)$ by A2. The rule returns $y_0$, which is correct. Contribution $p_0(1-\delta)$.

\emph{(ii) Gate passes, base incorrect.} $\Pr[C(y_0){=}0,V(y_0){=}1]=(1-p_0)\varepsilon$. The rule returns $y_0$, which is incorrect. Contribution $0$.

\emph{(iii) Gate fails.} $\beta:=\Pr[V(y_0){=}0]=p_0\delta+(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)$, and within this event $\Pr[C(y_0){=}1\mid V(y_0){=}0]=p_0\delta/\beta$. By A1 the $N$ candidates are independent of $y_0$, and by A2 each is accepted with probability $q=p(1-\delta)+(1-p)\varepsilon$ independently, so $\Pr[\mathcal{A}=\emptyset]=(1-q)^N$.
If $\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset$, Lemma~\ref{lem:precision} gives that the returned candidate is correct with probability $r=p(1-\delta)/q$, contributing $\beta\bigl(1-(1-q)^N\bigr)r$.
If $\mathcal{A}=\emptyset$ the rule returns $y_0$, correct with probability $p_0\delta/\beta$, contributing $\beta(1-q)^N\cdot p_0\delta/\beta=p_0\delta(1-q)^N$.

Summing (i)--(iii) gives the stated expression. Setting $N=0$ yields $p_0(1-\delta)+p_0\delta=p_0$, as required. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:gain} (gain decomposition).}
Put $\delta=0$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:master}. Then $q=p+(1-p)\varepsilon$, $r=p/q$, the term $p_0\delta(1-q)^N$ vanishes, and $\beta=(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)$, giving the displayed form.
\emph{Monotone in $N$:} $1-(1-q)^N$ is non-decreasing in $N$ because $0\le1-q\le1$, and every other factor is a non-negative constant.
\emph{Monotone in $\varepsilon$:} write $A_\infty-p_0=(1-p_0)h(\varepsilon)$ with $h(\varepsilon)=(1-\varepsilon)p/\bigl(p+(1-p)\varepsilon\bigr)$. Differentiating and using $p+(1-p)=1$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
h'(\varepsilon)&=\frac{-p\bigl(p+(1-p)\varepsilon\bigr)-p(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)}{\bigl(p+(1-p)\varepsilon\bigr)^{2}}\\[2pt]
&=\frac{-p}{\bigl(p+(1-p)\varepsilon\bigr)^{2}}\;<\;0
\end{aligned}
\]
\hfill$\square$

\begin{corollary}[Degradation]\label{cor:degrade}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:model} with $\delta>0$, write
$q=p(1-\delta)+(1-p)\varepsilon$ and let $r=p(1-\delta)/q$ be the chance an
accepted candidate is correct. Accuracy falls below the base exactly when
\[
\underbrace{p_0\,\delta\,(1-r)}_{\text{correct outputs thrown away}}\;>\;
\underbrace{(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)\,r}_{\text{incorrect ones caught}} .
\]
At $\delta=0$ the left side vanishes and Cor.~\ref{cor:gain} is recovered.
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:degrade} (degradation).}
Let $N\to\infty$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:master}. If $q>0$ then $(1-q)^N\to0$ and $A_\infty=p_0(1-\delta)+\beta r$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_\infty-p_0&=-p_0\delta+\bigl[p_0\delta+(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)\bigr]r\\[2pt]
&=-p_0\delta(1-r)+(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)r,
\end{aligned}
\]
and $A_\infty<p_0$ iff $p_0\delta(1-r)>(1-p_0)(1-\varepsilon)r$. If $q=0$ then no candidate is ever accepted, the rule always returns $y_0$, and $A_\infty=p_0$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:hetero} (heterogeneous gain).}
Assume A1--A2 conditionally on each instance with rates $p_0(x),p(x),\varepsilon(x)$ and $\delta(x)=0$, and drop A3. Corollary~\ref{cor:gain} applied conditionally on $x$ at $N=\infty$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr[C(\hat y){=}1\mid x]&=p_0(x)+\bigl(1-p_0(x)\bigr)h(x),\\
h(x)&=\tfrac{(1-\varepsilon(x))p(x)}{q(x)} .
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking expectations and using $\mathbb{E}[UW]=\mathbb{E}[U]\mathbb{E}[W]+\operatorname{Cov}(U,W)$ with $U=1-p_0(x)$ and $W=h(x)$,
\[A_\infty=\bar p_0+(1-\bar p_0)\mathbb{E}[h]+\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(1-p_0(x),h(x)\bigr).\]
Only the covariance identity is used, so no independence or convexity hypothesis enters. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:jensen} (pooled rates are conservative).}
Assume in addition $p(x)\equiv p\in(0,1)$, so $h(x)=h(\varepsilon(x))$ with $h$ as in Corollary~\ref{cor:gain}. Differentiating $h'$ above,
\[h''(\varepsilon)=\frac{2p(1-p)}{\bigl(p+(1-p)\varepsilon\bigr)^{3}}>0 ,\]
so $h$ is strictly convex on $[0,1]$ and Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb{E}[h(\varepsilon)]\ge h(\bar\varepsilon)$. Since $\widehat G=(1-\bar p_0)h(\bar\varepsilon)$, substituting into Proposition~\ref{prop:hetero} yields the claim. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Remark (the hypothesis in Corollary~\ref{cor:jensen} is necessary).}
If $p$ also varies, $h$ is a function of two arguments and is \emph{not} jointly convex, so $\mathbb{E}[h(p,\varepsilon)]\ge h(\bar p,\bar\varepsilon)$ may fail and with it the inequality. Appendix~\ref{app:numeric} exhibits a heterogeneous population with varying $p$ in which the direction reverses. We therefore state Corollary~\ref{cor:jensen} under constant $p$ rather than patching it.

\section{Auxiliary lemma}\label{app:lemma}
\begin{lemma}[Acceptance precision]\label{lem:precision}
Under A1--A2, let $\mathcal{A}=\{i\in[N]:V(y_i)=1\}$ and let $I$ be uniform on $\mathcal{A}$. Then, with $q=p(1-\delta)+(1-p)\varepsilon$ and $r=p(1-\delta)/q$,
\[\Pr\bigl[C(y_I)=1 \bigm| \mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset\bigr]=r .\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Under A1--A2 the pairs $\bigl(C(y_i),V(y_i)\bigr)$ are i.i.d.\ across $i$, with $\Pr[V{=}1]=q$ and $\Pr[C{=}1,V{=}1]=p(1-\delta)$, hence $\Pr[C{=}1\mid V{=}1]=r$. Fix a non-empty $A\subseteq[N]$ and condition on $\{\mathcal{A}=A\}$. This event constrains $V(y_i)=1$ for $i\in A$ and $V(y_i)=0$ for $i\notin A$, and by independence across $i$ it leaves $\{C(y_i)\}_{i\in A}$ i.i.d.\ with law $\Pr[C{=}1\mid V{=}1]$. Therefore $\Pr[C(y_I){=}1\mid\mathcal{A}=A]=r$ for every such $A$, and averaging over non-empty $A$ preserves the value.
\end{proof}
